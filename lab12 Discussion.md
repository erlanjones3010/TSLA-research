# Lab 12 — Tesla Full Analysis

## 1. Target Selection

I selected Tesla because it has an interesting mix of risk and growth. Its automotive business has recently struggled, while its energy business is growing and the company continues investing heavily in AI, autonomy, robotics, batteries, and other technology.

Tesla is a good company to analyze because several different parts of the business can affect its future value. Automotive is still its largest business, but energy and technology investments could become more important over time. This makes assumptions about revenue growth, margins, R&D, and capital spending important.

My initial view was that Tesla had strong long-term growth opportunities, but its valuation appeared difficult to justify using traditional automotive and cash-flow measures.

The main question I wanted to answer was: Can Tesla generate enough future growth, margins, and free cash flow to support its valuation?

## 2. Company and Evidence

Tesla primarily makes money from selling vehicles, but it also generates revenue from energy generation and storage, services, and other businesses. Automotive remains the largest portion of revenue, while energy has become an important source of growth.

| Metric | FY2025 Result | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Revenue | $94.8B | Down about 2.9% |
| Automotive revenue | $69.5B | 73.3% of revenue and down 10% |
| Energy revenue | $12.8B | 13.5% of revenue and up 27% |
| Energy gross margin | 29.8% | Strong profitability in a growing segment |
| Net income | $3.8B | About a 4.0% net margin |
| Operating cash flow | $14.7B | About 15.5% of revenue |
| Capex | $8.5B | About 9.0% of revenue |
| Cash + short-term investments | $44.1B | Strong liquidity |
| Debt | $8.2B | Relatively small compared with liquidity |

The evidence that mattered most to my analysis was the difference between automotive and energy. Tesla's total revenue declined about 3%, and automotive revenue declined 10%. At the same time, energy revenue grew 27% and had a 29.8% gross margin.

**Main takeaway:** Tesla has significant growth opportunities, especially outside its traditional automotive business, but those opportunities need to offset weakness in automotive. Tesla also has strong liquidity, but its large investment requirements make future cash generation important.

## 4. Valuation

I used multiple valuation methods rather than relying on only one method.

| Valuation Method | Value Per Share |
|---|---:|
| Qualified-peer P/E | $28.28 |
| DCF | $38.82 |
| Five-year FCFE pro-forma | $31.14 |
| Saved market price used in my analysis | $356.09 |

My DCF estimates value based on the future cash flows Tesla could generate. Those future cash flows are discounted back to the present because future money is worth less than money today.

I used a 10% cost of equity and a 3% terminal growth rate.

My valuation methods produce different results because they measure Tesla differently. The peer P/E method uses an earnings multiple from a comparable company. The DCF values future cash flows. The five-year FCFE model uses my detailed financial forecast and the cash flows available to equity holders.

GM was the qualified GAAP-EPS peer used in my peer P/E analysis. However, this method has an important limitation because Tesla has businesses and expected growth opportunities outside traditional automotive manufacturing.

My modeled values of approximately $28–$39 per share are substantially below the $356.09 saved market price used in my analysis.

This does not mean Tesla's stock must fall to my modeled values. Instead, my conventional automotive and cash-flow valuation assumptions do not explain the much higher market valuation. A substantially higher valuation would require additional economic value from areas such as energy, AI, autonomy, robotics, or stronger future growth that my conventional valuation approaches do not fully capture.

**Reverse DCF:** Unresolved.

## 5. Sensitivity and Drivers

My Lab 11 sensitivity analysis tested two operating drivers:

1. Revenue growth
2. R&D as a percentage of revenue

| Driver | Sensitivity Change | Value Per Share Range | Change vs. $31.14 Base |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue growth | ±3 percentage points | $27.32–$35.27 | -12.3% to +13.3% |
| R&D / Revenue | ±1 percentage point | $26.44–$35.84 | -15.1% to +15.1% |

Over these tested ranges, R&D produced the larger effect on value per share.

R&D changes  
→ Operating expenses change  
→ Operating income changes  
→ FCFE changes  
→ Value per share changes

If R&D increases while everything else stays constant, operating expenses increase. This lowers operating income and ultimately lowers FCFE and modeled value per share.

Revenue growth had a larger effect on operating income. However, higher revenue growth also increased working-capital requirements in my model. Therefore, not all of the additional operating earnings translated into additional FCFE.

This conclusion applies **over these ranges**. The sensitivity analysis does not prove that R&D is always more important than revenue growth. The size of the sensitivity range affects the size of the resulting change, so different ranges could change the ranking.

A sensitivity analysis is not a probability forecast. It shows how the model reacts when an assumption changes, not how likely that change is to happen.

## 6. Interpretation

My analysis shows a large difference between Tesla's saved market price and the values produced by the conventional valuation methods I used. My models therefore require substantially stronger future economic performance or additional value from areas such as energy, AI, autonomy, and robotics to explain the market valuation used in my analysis.

Evidence that would strengthen my valuation case includes:

- Sustained improvement in automotive deliveries and pricing
- Higher automotive margins
- Continued strong energy-storage growth and margins
- Evidence that AI, autonomy, Robotaxi, and robotics generate meaningful revenue and cash flow
- Higher operating cash flow and FCFE as Tesla's investments mature
- Evidence that Tesla's high capital spending produces attractive returns

Evidence that would make me more concerned includes:

- Continued automotive volume or pricing weakness
- Further margin deterioration
- Slower energy growth or lower energy margins
- R&D and AI spending increasing without measurable financial returns
- High capital spending failing to produce future cash flow
- More financing or dilution than my model assumes

My view of Tesla changed during the project. Initially, I focused heavily on Tesla's growth opportunities. After completing my valuation and pro-forma, I realized that growth alone is not enough. Tesla eventually needs that growth to translate into stronger margins and free cash flow.

The energy business also became more important to my analysis because it was growing while automotive revenue was declining.

The main area I would research next is whether Tesla's R&D and capital spending are producing measurable returns. I would specifically watch whether investments in AI, autonomy, robotics, batteries, and energy eventually lead to higher revenue, margins, operating cash flow, and FCFE.

## Final Conclusion

I chose Tesla because it has an interesting mix of a weakening automotive business and potential growth from energy, AI, autonomy, and robotics. My research showed that automotive revenue declined while energy grew strongly, creating a mixed outlook. I built a five-year forecast assuming revenue and margins recover while Tesla continues spending heavily on R&D and capital investments. My valuation methods produced values between approximately $28 and $39 per share compared with the $356.09 market price used in my analysis. In my sensitivity analysis, R&D had the largest effect on value per share over the ranges I tested. The biggest thing that could change my conclusion would be evidence that Tesla's investments in energy, AI, autonomy, and robotics are producing sustainable revenue, stronger margins, and free cash flow.

Revenue Growth

SG&A as a percentage of revenue affects the model through this path:

SG&A % of Revenue  
→ SG&A Expense  
→ Operating Profit

A higher SG&A percentage means higher operating expenses, which lowers operating profit and cash flow. A lower SG&A percentage improves operating profitability and cash flow, holding the other independent assumptions constant.
